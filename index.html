Miscalibrated Judges: A Psychometric Evaluation of LLMs as Hate Speech Raters

Mikel K. Ngueajio1, Rick Battle2, NaheedAnjum Arafat1, Danda B. Rawat1,
1Howard University 2VMWARE NLP Lab
Draft Paper Code Data

Problem

LLMs are increasingly used to scale content moderation, but can we trust them to understand hate speech the way humans do? Human annotations are costly and inconsistent, while AI systems introduce new forms of bias and disagreement. This work systematically examines human and LLM annotation behaviors to uncover where they align, where they diverge, and what this means for trustworthy moderation.

Abstract

Our findings reveal that,

  • LLMs are systematically more lenient than human annotators, underestimating implicit hate while overestimating explicit hate—the very cases where early moderation matters most.
  • Prompting strategy matters. Modular prompting increases leniency while preserving ranking consistency, with DeepSeek showing the greatest sensitivity to prompting design.
  • Raw LLM scores can be misleading. After psychometric calibration, most LLMs receive higher hate severity scores, highlighting the need for formal severity adjustment before comparison with humans.
  • Human and LLM agreement diverge. The hate speech dimension with the highest human agreement exhibits the greatest disagreement across LLMs, motivating calibrated alignment with human measurement standards.

We investigate four research questions across ten hate speech dimensions.

Key Findings

Finding 1: LLMs Are Systematic but Miscalibrated Raters

Most LLM configurations are systematically more lenient than human annotators — all except GPT-5 fall below the human mean severity. Yet all LLM configurations cluster within ~2.0 logits, occupying less than 25% of the 8-logit human severity range. This suggests LLMs are systematically biased than human raters. We believe this bias can in principle be corrected through psychometric calibration. Moreover, we notice that 6 out of of 9 LLM configurations outperform the human baseline on Infit Mean Statistics, demonstrating that carefully configured LLMs can apply psychometric scales more consistently than crowd-sourced human annotators.

Finding 2: Consistent Dimension Difficulty Ordering

The relative difficulty of hate speech dimensions is preserved across all model families and prompting strategies. Sentiment, Insult, Respect, and Attack-Defend are consistently the easiest dimensions to endorse, while Dehumanization, Genocide, and Violence are consistently the hardest. Human-LLM item difficulty correlations range from r = 0.834 to 0.948 (all p < .001), confirming that LLMs share humans' understanding of which hate speech constructs are most difficult to endorse.

Finding 3: The Dehumanization Blind Spot

LLMs systematically miss subtle dehumanization. While humans place dehumanization at δi = 0.485, all LLM configurations place it substantially higher (δi = 1.160–1.855), indicating they require far more extreme content before flagging dehumanizing rhetoric — precisely the dimension most linked to real-world escalation pathways. At the same time, Violence and Genocide show substantial human-LLM divergence, meaning they require subjective severity judgment that neither humans nor LLMs resolve consistently. The three-class Hate Speech label, despite showing the highest human inter-rater reliability (according to Sachdeva at al, 2022) — produces the greatest cross-model LLM disagreement (Δδ = 1.510 logits), underscoring that high human agreement does not guarantee consistent LLM behavior.

Finding 4: Prompting Strategy Materially Affects Severity

Prompting strategy produces meaningful shifts in absolute severity while preserving rank-order agreement. Modular prompting; despite being the most resource intensive, produces higher severity ratings than Monolithic prompting, with absolute differences ranging from 0.24 logits (Gemini) to 0.79 logits (GPT-5-mini). We attribute this to the cross-dimensional anchoring effect: under monolithic prompting, evaluating all dimensions simultaneously allows ratings to anchor each other, yielding more conservative individual ratings.

Finding 3: Cross Cultural Model Comparison.

DeepSeek (mono) falls close to the human mean and performs comparably to Claude mono and Gemini mono under monolithic prompting. consistent with our hypothesis and smiliar scholarly work which posit that pretraining data convergence across model families thus producing similar severity profiles regardless of model origin. However, DeepSeek (modu) shows disproportionate sensitivity suggesting that cultural training differences may surface only when cross-dimensional anchoring is removed.

Finding 6: Psychometric Validation and Reliability

To validate our framework, we compute separation reliability and item-level fit statistics from the joint MFRM calibration. Separation reliability far exceeds the recommended threshold of 0.80 (Item: 0.996, Person: 0.989), confirming that observed differences between raters and dimensions reflect genuine measurement phenomena rather than noise. Joint MFRM calibration achieves r = 0.819 with the Kennedy et al. gold standard (p < .001, MAE = 0.979), validating RaschPy as an open-source alternative to closed-source Winsteps for reproducible hate speech psychometrics.
