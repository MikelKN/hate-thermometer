Miscalibrated Judges: A Psychometric Evaluation of LLMs as Hate Speech Raters

Mikel K. Ngueajio1,2, Rick Battle3, NaheedAnjum Arafat1, Danda B. Rawat1,
1Howard University 2CMU Africa 3VMWARE NLP Lab
Paper AAAI AIES Code Data

Problem

LLMs are increasingly used to scale content moderation, but can we trust them to understand hate speech the way humans do? Human annotations are costly and inconsistent, while AI systems introduce new forms of bias and disagreement. This work systematically examines human and LLM annotation behaviors to uncover where they align, where they diverge, and what this means for trustworthy moderation.

Abstract

Our findings reveal that,

  • LLMs are systematically more lenient than human annotators, underestimating implicit hate while overestimating explicit hate—the very cases where early moderation matters most.
  • Prompting strategy matters. Modular prompting increases leniency while preserving ranking consistency, with DeepSeek showing the greatest sensitivity to prompting design.
  • Raw LLM scores can be misleading. After psychometric calibration, most LLMs receive higher hate severity scores, highlighting the need for formal severity adjustment before comparison with humans.
  • Human and LLM agreement diverge. The hate speech dimension with the highest human agreement exhibits the greatest disagreement across LLMs, motivating calibrated alignment with human measurement standards.

Key Findings

Finding 1: LLMs are more lenient raters than humans.

All LLMs except GPT-5 underestimate hate severity. Yet after joint MFRM calibration, lenient models produce higher calibrated hate scores — because MFRM interprets high scores from lenient raters as strong evidence of true hate severity.

Finding 2: Prompting strategy effects.

Prompting strategy produces meaningful shifts in absolute severity rating while preserving rank-order agreement. Modular prompting despite bieng the most resource costly, produces higher severity ratings than Monolithic prompting, with absolute severity differences ranging from 0.24 logits (Gemini) to 0.79 logits (GPT-5-mini). We attribute this pattern to the halo effect (Engelhard Jr 1994). This finding demonstrates that prompt structure materially influences LLM annotation behavior.

Finding 3: Western Vs non Western Model.

DeepSeek mono falls close to the human mean and performs comparably to Claude mono, Gemini mono and with an absolute mono-modu logit difference comparable to those of Gemini, consistent with H3, which predicted alignment across Western and non-Western model

Finding 4: The Dehumanization Blind Spot and Violence, Genocide Divergence

LLMs systematically miss subtle DEHUMNAIZATION. Human threshold: δ = 0.485. LLM threshold: δ = 1.160–1.855. LLMs require far more extreme content before flagging dehumanizing rhetoric. Similarly, VIOLENCE and GENO- CIDE show substantial human-LLM divergence, suggesting these dimensions require subjective severity judgment that neither humans nor LLMs resolve consistently.

Finding 5: LLM Alignment to Human Crowd-Workers

  • LLMs preserve human-like ranking of hate severity but show systematic leniency. MFRM calibration corrects these rater effects, revealing that raw LLM scores are not directly comparable to human judgments without accounting for differences in annotation behavior.

Finding 6: LLM not fit for standalone moderation, but can be used to augment human moderation.

LLM annotation unreliability is concentrated at two intersecting points: ambiguous dimensions (e.g HUMILIATION, STATUS, DEHUMANIZATION) and low-severity comments. Both are precisely the cases most relevant to early-stage hate speech intervention, supporting RQ1
